We’re revisiting Silicon Roundabout in the age of AI, and the changes begin before we leave Old Street station. The [roundabout has become a peninsula](https://bregroup.com/case-studies/old-st-roundabout-awarded-breeam-infrastructure-excellent), with new entrances and public space. Its familiar nickname has outlasted the road layout that inspired it.

In 2008, [Matt Biddulph mapped just 15 nearby technology businesses](https://www.wired.com/story/oral-history-london-tech-scene/). Two years later, the government’s [Tech City initiative](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/pm-announces-east-london-tech-city) attached national ambitions to this corner of east London. As we return to the area today, AI gives us a reason to look again at what supports the businesses growing here.

<!--read-->

#### Colt and the connections behind AI

When walking the streets between co-working spaces and offices around Old Street and Shoreditch, we're beginning to think that services developed inside them may depend on computing resources across several countries. Data must travel between customers, business systems, and the facilities that process it. The network helps make a service feel immediate, even when the work happens far away.

That brings us to Colt. On 12 October, the company announced [further investment in its global network](https://www.colt.net/it/resources/insights/colt-expands-global-network-investment-to-power-the-next-wave-of-ai-growth), expanding its reach across North America, Africa, Asia and the Middle East alongside European upgrades. The programme includes a new long-distance route in Japan and fibre improvements between Marseille and Milan. Colt says its network connects 1,100 data centres worldwide.

Colt’s Joe Scattareggia describes the task as “connecting data, cloud and compute at the scale AI demands.” For a business adding AI to an existing service, that raises practical questions. Can information move quickly enough? Will the connection remain dependable as usage grows? What happens when a route is disrupted?

Colt’s expansion means investing in connections allows digital businesses to operate beyond their office walls. Its announcement concerns a global network, and that international reach is precisely what makes it relevant to a neighbourhood built around businesses with ambitions beyond London.

#### Truman Brewery and the space behind our screens

Truman Brewery estate brings another part of this infrastructure story into view. In July 2026, the government [approved a 5,200-square-metre data centre at 47 Grey Eagle Street](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/recovered-appeal-sites-on-and-around-the-site-of-truman-brewery-tower-hamlets-london-refs-3367041-3367172-3367179-and-3367369-29-july-2026), alongside the wider redevelopment. Intended to provide shared server space principally for financial businesses, the facility would add capacity to store and process data close to its users. Alongside Colt’s network expansion, it gives us another way to understand the physical requirements of a digital economy. AI’s economic impact is also attracting attention internationally: in September 2025, [Goldman Sachs estimated that AI had added around $160 billion to US real economic activity since 2022](https://www.businessinsider.com/ai-tech-economy-us-gdp-boost-chips-blindspot-goldman-sachs-2025-9), equivalent to approximately 0.7% of US GDP. Back in East London, the brewery proposals bring the discussion down to street level, where demand for computing space is becoming part of the debate about the neighbourhood’s future.

#### Young users and everyday AI

Younger users are already part of this changing picture. In 2025, [79% of UK 16–24-year-olds used AI tools](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-adults/passive-social-media-use-ai-companionship-and-online-side-hustles-uk-adults-media-and-online-lives-revealed), compared with 54% of adults overall. Alongside that deliberate use, AI is becoming part of services people use without necessarily noticing the technology underneath.

A routine web search provides another point of contact. In the same research, 62% of internet users aged 16–24 said they read AI-generated search summaries often or always. These summaries pull information from across the web and present it in search results. Even among people who did not use AI chatbots, 54% said they read these summaries at least sometimes, showing how interaction with AI extends beyond opening a dedicated chatbot.

For younger users and the rest of us, AI’s most visible features are the tools on our screens. The fibre networks connecting businesses, cloud services and data centres are harder to see, yet they help make those everyday experiences possible. Colt’s upgrades are part of this less visible story.
