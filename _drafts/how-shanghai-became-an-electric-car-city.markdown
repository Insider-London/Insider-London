In November 2016, a Shanghai company brought an electric supercar to London’s Saatchi Gallery. Its founder, William Li, was there to introduce a name few British drivers had heard before: NIO. The car on display, the EP9, had already broken an [electric lap record](https://www.nio.com/news/nextev-launches-nio-brand-and-worlds-fastest-electric-car) in Germany. Getting people to buy an electric car for the school run or a long journey home would be a different challenge. 

Ten years on, **Insider London is partnering with NIO in Shanghai as part of our Shanghai business programme**. For visiting students and business groups, NIO offers a way into a bigger story. How did a city known for building petrol cars become a place where companies are rethinking what it means to own and drive an electric one?

<!--read-->

#### Shanghai was a car city long before NIO

In 1983, workers in Shanghai assembled China’s first [Volkswagen Santana](https://volkswagengroupchina.com.cn/en/volkswagengroupchina/chronology). By the time the city hosted the World Expo in 2010, visitors could see a different kind of vehicle taking shape. Sunwin, a Shanghai joint venture between Volvo and the Chinese carmaker SAIC, supplied 120 battery-powered buses for the event. Another 36 could recharge during the brief time they spent at bus stops. Years before NIO unveiled its supercar, Shanghai was already testing a question that still matters to drivers today: when and where do you recharge? 

Those decades of car-making gave new companies something valuable: suppliers close enough to work with in person. Shanghai now has more than 600 major automotive parts businesses. The city estimates that manufacturers can source 80% of the parts they need within an hour’s drive. There’s a British link here, too. [SAIC owns MG](https://www.saicmotor.com/english/history/r1.html). [english.shanghai.gov.cn](https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-KeyIndustries/20260922/be4cb52fc12747be9a27a6bf5a8e4013.html), so a badge familiar to generations of British drivers is also part of Shanghai’s automotive history.

#### Why NIO chose to swap the battery

Before founding NIO in Shanghai in 2014, William Li ran a website that helped sell petrol cars. In a [later interview](https://perkinswill.com/insights/ev-automaker-nio-house-carbon-neutral-architecture/), he recalled thinking about the air his future children would breathe and his own part in promoting those cars. By 2018, NIO’s first production model, the ES8, was on the road. Its owners had an unusual option when the battery ran low. They could pull into a station and exchange it for a charged one. 

That choice changed the business as well as the car. In 2020, NIO introduced a battery subscription. Customers could buy the vehicle at a lower upfront price and pay monthly for the battery. For drivers, the appeal depends partly on whether there’s a station where they need one. For NIO, it means having charged batteries ready long after the original sale. In February 2026, Li joined customers and employees in Shanghai to watch the company record its [100 millionth battery swap](https://www.nio.com/news/20260206001). NIO says an exchange takes about [three minutes](https://www.nio.com/news/nio-launches-battery-service) on average.

#### Where electric cars stand today

Worldwide, one in four new cars sold in 2025 was electric. In China, the share was already close to 55%, according to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/executive-summary). Those figures include fully electric cars and plug-in hybrids. For a carmaker in Shanghai, that changes the sales conversation. Many customers are already considering an electric car. The question is what would make them choose yours. 

In the first ten months of 2026, global sales of electric vehicles rose 5% compared with the same period in 2025. In China, EV sales fell 11%, but the wider car market shrank faster, so electric models still accounted for a larger share of new car sales. For drivers weighing up which electric car to buy, NIO offers one practical difference. At its battery-swap stations, they can exchange a depleted battery for a charged one in minutes.
